    <h3 class="publication-title">
      Human Oversight of AI Redistributive Decisions
      <span class="publication-badge">Job Market Paper</span>
    </h3>

    <p class="publication-meta">
      Damiano Paoli
    </p>

    <details class="publication-details">
      <summary class="publication-summary">Abstract</summary>
      <div class="publication-abstract">
        <p>
          Artificial intelligence is increasingly integrated into high-stakes decisions,
          from benefits allocation to hiring and healthcare, promising substantial
          efficiency gains. Human oversight of consequential AI decisions is already
          required by law, but its effectiveness is not guaranteed. If individuals are
          skeptical about AI judgment, they may scrutinize AI decisions excessively,
          offsetting the efficiency gains that motivated adoption; if they defer
          automatically, the oversight mechanism fails.
        </p>
        <p>
          This paper investigates whether people oversee other-regarding decisions made by
          an artificial agent differently from the same decisions made by a human. I develop
          a theoretical framework for how people revise redistributive decisions made by
          humans and by artificial agents, and test its predictions in two pre-registered
          online experiments with samples representative of the U.S. population. In both
          experiments, two workers initially receive unequal earnings, either because one
          performed better in a real-effort task or because one won a lottery, and a
          third-party spectator, either a human or an artificial agent based on a large
          language model, can redistribute the workers' earnings. Then, another impartial
          participant, the reviewer, observes the allocation made by the spectator and
          decides whether to pay a fee to overturn it. In the first experiment, reviewers do
          not know whether the inequality came from merit or luck; in the second, they do.
        </p>
        <p>
          In both experiments, reviewers intervene more when the fairness loss from leaving
          the allocation in place is higher, but they do not intervene more when the
          spectator is an AI, ruling out AI aversion. Human oversight is therefore unlikely
          to generate excessive scrutiny driven by an intrinsic reluctance to defer to
          algorithmic judgment, though for the same reason overseers cannot be expected to
          be more vigilant just because the decision-maker is a machine.
        </p>
      </div>
    </details>

    <h3 class="publication-title">
      Coordination and Leadership: The Impact of Artificial Intelligence
    </h3>

    <p class="publication-meta">
      <a href="https://www.unibo.it/sitoweb/maria.bigoni/en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">
        Maria Bigoni
      </a>, Damiano Paoli
    </p>

    <p class="publication-meta"><em>Journal of Economic Behavior &amp; Organization, 2026.</em></p>

    <details class="publication-details">
      <summary class="publication-summary">Abstract</summary>
      <div class="publication-abstract">
        <p>
          Coordination failure is an obstacle to collective efficiency in many
          economic and social settings. Leadership can mitigate this problem by
          providing a focal point through public communication, but the
          effectiveness of such communication relies on shared trust that the
          leader's recommendations will be followed by others.
        </p>
        <p>
          As generative artificial intelligence (AI) becomes increasingly
          accessible, leaders may delegate communication tasks to automated
          systems. Whether such delegation weakens the coordinating role of
          leadership remains an open question. We investigate this issue through
          an online experiment based on the minimum effort game with leadership,
          where leaders could send either a human-written or a ChatGPT-generated
          message and followers were informed of the message's source.
        </p>
        <p>
          Results show that coordination outcomes do not differ across treatments:
          AI- and human-generated messages are equally effective in promoting
          coordination on the Pareto-dominant equilibrium. Beliefs about other
          players' behavior are also unaffected by the source of the message,
          suggesting that delegation to AI does not undermine strategic
          communication or followers' trust in successful coordination.
        </p>
      </div>
    </details>

    <h3 class="publication-title">
      Favouring Tax Compliance Through a Simple Automatic Payment Option: Evidence from a Lab Experiment
    </h3>

    <p class="publication-meta">
      Domenico Colucci, Patrizia Lattarulo, Damiano Paoli, Maria Grazia Pazienza, Vincenzo Valori
    </p>

    <p class="publication-meta"><em>Italian Economic Journal, 2026.</em></p>

    <details class="publication-details">
      <summary class="publication-summary">Abstract</summary>
      <div class="publication-abstract">
        <p>
          This study investigates whether reducing compliance costs can improve tax
          compliance. We focus on the impact of automatic payment systems on tax
          evasion, with the aim of providing evidence relevant for minor indirect
          taxes, such as the vehicle tax collected by regional governments in Italy.
        </p>
        <p>
          We design a laboratory experiment in which subjects play a Tax Evasion
          Game. Participants receive a fixed endowment and perform a real-effort task
          under time pressure, during which they can earn additional income but are
          required to pay a tax. While evasion yields a higher expected payoff than
          compliance, it entails the risk of a substantial fine if detected.
        </p>
        <p>
          In the treatment condition, we introduce an automatic payment option that
          allows participants to commit to paying the tax before starting the task.
          We find that reducing the non-monetary burden of compliance significantly
          increases tax compliance. Finally, we elicit individual risk preferences
          and show that they do not account for the treatment effect.
        </p>
      </div>
    </details>
